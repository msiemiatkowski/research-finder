How Research Finder works

What it does for your research

What are you working on?

The whole search, from the first search string to the search appendix

A review is only as complete as its search. A wording or a language the search leaves out is a study the review, and every pooled estimate in it, leaves out.

  • A search string built from the literature

    For each keyword it reads the 200 best-matching studies and collects the other wordings they use. You keep or drop every term.

  • Past the language bias

    Every term is searched in 39 more languages. Each translation is checked by translating it back, and you see all of them.

  • Grey literature, against publication bias

    Preprints, theses, reports, study registrations and datasets are searched next to journal articles.

  • Records ready for screening

    Every record with its abstract, each study once: a Google Sheet for your team and a RIS file for Rayyan, Covidence, ASReview, Zotero or EndNote.

  • Full texts and data for coding

    A link to every open PDF, the files themselves where they can be fetched, and open datasets for effect sizes a paper does not report.

  • The search, documented

    Sources, date, every term and translation, filters, the exact queries and the record counts: what PRISMA 2020 items 6 and 7 ask for. Run the same queries again to update the review.

  • A head start in your other databases

    Copy the Boolean string to adapt for Scopus, Web of Science or PsycINFO, or open it in PubMed, Google Scholar or BASE.

Map a topic quickly: for a paper, a thesis or a grant proposal

Before you can say what is known, you need to see how big the topic is, who shaped it and where it is moving.

  • See how big a topic is

    Each keyword shows how many studies have it in their title or abstract, before you read a single one.

  • Start from the existing reviews

    One filter shows the review articles: 69 of them for “trust in science”.

  • Landmark papers and the newest ones

    Sort by citations or by date, and filter by year, type and open access.

  • Find the neighboring topics

    Related phrases, the studies’ own keywords and the topics most often studied with yours are one click away.

  • Read and cite from one place

    Every result with its abstract in one sheet, and a RIS file that loads the references into Zotero, EndNote or Mendeley.

Open datasets to reanalyze, replicate or teach with

The data behind published studies is scattered over repositories that each have their own search. Here it is one search, and the files come with it.

  • Five repositories in one search

    DataCite, Zenodo, Harvard Dataverse, OSF and OpenAIRE: 629 datasets and registrations for “trust in science”.

  • The files, not just the links

    One Drive folder per dataset with its open data files, and a sheet that lists every file with its size and download link.

  • Licenses and DOIs

    Both are in the results sheet, so that you can reuse the data properly and cite it.

  • Preregistrations too

    OSF registrations show what was planned, including studies that were never published.

  • A folder your team already knows how to use

    A saved search is an ordinary Google Drive folder: share it with co-authors or with a class.

The studies an English-only search never sees

Much research is published in the language of the people it studies, in national journals that the big databases index thinly.

  • Research published in other languages

    346 of the 3,402 studies on “trust in science” are not in English. They are in more than 25 other languages.

  • Adapted and validated measures

    The validation study of a questionnaire in another language is often published in that language, in a national journal.

  • Local evidence

    Filter by the language of the study to see what has been published in Polish, Portuguese or Korean.

  • You stay in control

    Every translation is shown and can be switched off, one term or one language at a time. Doubtful machine translations are left out unless you add them.

How it works

Every step is shown, and you can change any of them

  1. It reads how studies word your topic

    For each keyword: the titles, abstracts and keywords of the 200 studies in OpenAlex that match it best. Phrases that say the same in other words are added. Related phrases are offered, and Wikidata adds synonyms.

  2. It translates every term

    Into 39 languages: the names Wikidata has, and Wikimedia’s open machine translation for the rest. Each translation is translated back into English, and one that returns with another meaning is left out.

  3. It builds one Boolean query

    OR within a keyword, AND between keywords, searched in titles and abstracts, or in full texts too. Any term or language can be switched off.

  4. It asks open databases directly

    From your browser, with nothing in between. No subscription and no account are needed.

Questions researchers ask

What it does not do, and other things worth knowing

Does it replace Scopus, Web of Science or PsycINFO?

No. OpenAlex is broad and open, but a systematic review usually also searches the bibliographic databases of its field, such as PsycINFO, MEDLINE, Scopus or Web of Science. Use Research Finder next to them: it finds wordings, languages and grey literature those searches tend to miss, and the copied search string gives you a head start there.

How good are the translations?

They are machine translations, except where Wikidata has the name of a topic in a language. Each one is translated back into English, and those that return with another meaning are left out. That catches many poor translations, not all. Look through them before you rely on them in a review, and switch off any that do not fit.

Why are some PDFs saved and others only linked?

Most publishers do not let a web page download their files, even open-access ones, and neither do OSF and figshare. In one test a browser alone could save 39 of 599 open PDFs. A file relay, a small free program you set up once under Settings, raised that to 405. Publishers that let only browsers in, such as Elsevier, Wiley or SAGE, keep their PDFs as links even then. The sheet “Files” gives the link and the reason for each file, and “Earlier saves” can try the missing ones again later.

Is there a limit on searching?

OpenAlex gives everyone on a network a free daily allowance: about 100 requests, each bringing up to 200 results, and the first 10,000 results of a search. A free personal OpenAlex key, added under Settings, allows ten times more, reaches every result and lets a long search carry all its translations.

What does it search: titles, abstracts or full texts?

Titles and abstracts by default, which is what a systematic search usually reports. A study without an abstract in OpenAlex is then found only by its title. You can search full texts too: that finds more, less precisely.

Who can see my searches and my files?

Nobody here. The site has no server, no account and no tracking: your browser talks directly to OpenAlex, the data repositories and Google. Research Finder asks Google only for access to the files it creates itself, so it cannot see anything else in your Drive. The privacy policy has the details.

Does it work outside psychology and the social sciences?

Yes. OpenAlex and the data repositories cover every field, and the wordings are read from the studies of your own topic, whatever it is. Coverage differs by field: where a field relies on its own database, use that too.

How do I report a search made with it, and cite it?

Each saved folder has the document “Search details”: the sources, the date, every term and translation, the filters, the exact queries and the number of records. That is the material for a methods section and a search appendix. The citation for Research Finder is below.
